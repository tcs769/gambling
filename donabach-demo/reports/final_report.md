### 2. The tax audit

* **2.1 A sale checked by hand and by PolicyEngine.** The advisor holds $1,000,000 of stock bought for $400,000 and sells it all in a year with $150,000 of wages and $5,000 of qualified dividends: federal tax $130,735 (ordinary $15,340, gains $96,205 at 15% and 20%, investment income tax $19,190), Illinois $37,372.50 (no exemption above $500,000), $144,634.58 more than the same year without the sale. The engine, the simulator's own logged year (`tests/test_tax_audit.py`) and PolicyEngine-US all agree to the cent. Fifteen households now match PolicyEngine to the cent, three of them single filers.
* **2.2 and 2.3 One end valuation for everyone.** The flat 15% on unrealized gains, the flat 15% on gains realized to pay the last year's tax and fee, and the 15% credit for an unused loss carryforward are gone. Every strategy, and the household's starting wealth, is valued by the tax engine: the pre-tax balance withdrawn evenly over 20 years and the unrealized gain realized over 5, beside Social Security, interest and dividends, the carryforward offsetting the gains, last-year gains and withdrawals landing in the first year. Effect on the median advantage over the good RIA: sample +0.084, showcase +0.027 (it taxes large gains at 20% + 3.8% + 4.95% where the old rule took 15%, and a carryforward now offsets real gains instead of being credited at 15%).
* **2.4 Selling diagnostics** for the showcase and sample households and for both baselines: `reports/diagnostics.md` (stock sold by purpose with gains and capital-gains tax; the same sales under highest-cost-first and first-in-first-out; where the reserve is held and the ordinary tax and penalties on pre-tax spending; unrealized gains at 80 and the tax charged on them; lifetime tax by kind of income, interest now its own line). Identities are tested: the tax by kind sums to the tax paid, the tax by purpose to the tax on the gains, sold by purpose to the tape of sales, and a replay under the rule the run used reproduces its realized gains.
* **Found, not changed.** (a) Households with one person on file are taxed as married filing jointly (two Illinois exemptions, joint brackets): that is the spec's convention (a non-earning spouse), but Tanaka's card says "one earner" and the engine cannot tell a single parent from a couple with one earner. (b) PolicyEngine differs from the engine in two places the engine does not model: the alternative minimum tax at about $900,000 of income, and itemizing the Illinois income tax as a state and local deduction at incomes of about $500,000 (12.4, not built). Neither touches the showcase household.

### 3. The liability-matched reserve

The simulator now counts a *forced sale*: stocks sold to pay for goals or spending in a year they fell. Measured first, it showed the reserve leaking: the safe assets sat in the Roth or the pre-tax account while spending took the taxable account's stocks first, so even a 6-year reserve forced a sale in 97% of futures. Fixed (a gap in the mechanism, not a tuning): in a year stocks fell, the bonds and private credit of every account are spent first (92% to 0.3% of futures with a spending sale for the sample household). Then three more genuine gaps: goals due within two years are funded in the goal bucket whatever the market did; a household still building toward retirement gets its first two years of spending in safe assets whatever the market did; and the part of the reserve that pays for the years before 59 1/2 is held in the taxable account (pre-tax money costs a 10% penalty before then). The `liability_matched` style picks the reserve (2 to 12 years) with the most median wealth among the plans whose chance of a forced sale is at most 2% and whose chance of ruin is no higher than the good advisor's, checked on the search's 400 futures and 1,200 it never saw (checked on the 400 alone, the winner of many strategies passes the limit by luck and misses it on fresh futures: 2.0% became 4.1%).

| 300 households | Median advantage over the good RIA | Risk-adjusted | Chance of a forced sale (advisor 99.8%) | Households within the limits (forced sale 3% or less, ruin no likelier than the advisor's) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Equity-first (unconstrained) | +1.69% | +0.32% | 6.6% | 112 of 300 |
| Liability-matched | +1.67% | +0.41% | 1.9% | 221 of 300 |

By type, liability-matched: diversified +1.80%, concentrated +1.96%, large gains +1.93%, large purchase +1.47%, high earners +1.33%, early retirement +1.27%; households within the limits 48, 44, 49, 26, 31 and 23 of 50. ### Why it is not between 2 and 3%, and what I found when I looked

**For the showcase household it is** (+2.05% liability-matched, +2.10% equity-first) — but it was +2.23% before this update, and several genuine corrections made it smaller (below). **For the typical household it is not: +1.67 to +1.69%**, and the best strategy on the whole menu is +1.8 to 2.3% by type. Reading `reports/path_to_2_3_percent.md` (regenerated on this engine), for the sample household:

* The most median wealth any plan produces is **+1.90%**; Donabach's tools at the advisor's own allocation are +1.02%; the other +0.88% is simply holding 94% stocks against the advisor's 80% falling to 50%, which is more risk, not skill (risk-adjusted: −0.56% for that plan, +0.06% for the app's plan). Against a good advisor who holds more equity the gap shrinks (90/60: +1.61%; 100/75: +1.27%) and against one who holds less it grows (70/40: +2.23%; 60/30: +2.60%).
* Levers that would move it, none adopted: the fee (0.25%: +2.00%; 0.15%: +2.11%; 0.00%: +2.26%); the equity return (6.5%: +2.24%; 7.5%: +2.57%; 9.5%: +3.23%; 5.8% reaches 2.0% for the ceiling plan, 8.8% reaches 3.0%); a 24% or 32% flat tax on heirs' pre-tax money (+1.96%, +2.12%).
* **Four things I found by auditing rather than by looking for a larger number.** Each moved the result and I kept the direction it went:
  1. The reserve leaked: a "6-year reserve" sold stocks in 97% of futures because safe assets sat in the wrong accounts. Fixed (the reserve is now spent first in a down year, across accounts); the showcase gap fell 0.07 points when I did.
  2. The flat 15% end valuation understated what the advisor's large unrealized gains and pre-tax balances cost, but overstated the carryforward: the tax-engine valuation raised the sample household's gap (+0.084) and the showcase household's (+0.027).
  3. Social Security taxed by the real formula lowered the showcase gap by 0.12 points.
  4. The typical advisor's own costs raised the gap over the typical advisor by 0.6 to 1.0 points but not over the good RIA, which is the benchmark of the 2–3% target.
* **What is leaving the number low is the benchmark, not the engine's treatment of Donabach.** The good RIA charges 0.5 to 1.0%, harvests losses, and holds 80% falling to 50% stocks; for the showcase household the flat fee is worth 0.83 points (Shapley), the reserve and equity allocation 0.68 (more risk, not free), conversions, asset location and lots about 0.45 together. The plan is already holding 85% stocks while spending safe assets first; the engine finds no further source on the menu.
* **Assumptions that would, and that I did not use.** Private credit at 5% (+0.3 points), a fee of 0.25% (+0.1), behavioural coaching (Vanguard puts it at up to 1.5 points but says it is not additive; the engine computes none), a dynamic spending rule, diversifying the concentrated stock. * **Not computed that could lower or raise it:** 7.3, 9.2–9.6, 12.4–12.7, the adaptive planner in the maximum-gap menu, survivors for one-person households (taxed as a joint return), and any real fund or tax data (the whole world is simulated and fictional).

**Bottom line.** For the showcase household the engine gives +2.05 to 2.10% a year over the good RIA, inside 2 to 3% at the low end and sensitive to one assumption (private credit's return: 1.80% to 2.40%). For the typical household it gives about +1.7%. Reaching 2% for the typical household needs a lower fee, a higher equity return, a lower-equity advisor, or private credit returning about 5%; each is a different assumption, and none was made.

### 1. Tax lots

Built as you listed them: (1) **lot tracking**: the brokerage stock is the opening position (long-term) plus one lot for each simulated year, which takes that year's reinvested dividends, rebalancing purchases and savings at cost; sales, rebalancing, realizing gains, the advisory fee and forced sales all go through the lots, and loss harvesting still lowers every lot's basis in proportion; (2) **highest cost first for Donabach** (the lot bought at the highest price is sold first, so the least gain is realized per dollar); (3) **first in, first out for both advisors**; (4) **short-term gains taxed as ordinary income** for every strategy: a gain on a lot sold in the year it was bought is short-term, on one sold the next year half short-term (purchases and sales are spread through a year, so such a lot has been held more than a year half the time; this is a modeling choice, not a rule from the tax code), on an older lot long-term; the short-term part is ordinary income after short-term and long-term losses are netted (the tax engine's `short_term_gain`; Illinois and the investment income tax treat both kinds alike). Valuation at the horizon still taxes all unrealized gain at 15%, so a lot rule moves tax in time and between brackets; it does not remove any. The old behavior is kept as `lot_method="average"` (every sale at the account's average basis, every gain long-term), which is how every earlier result regenerates.

### 4. What it cost, what I fixed on the way, and what is not done

**Cost.** Lot tracking makes every simulated year do more arithmetic. Measured on 4 cores, each pair in one process with the rules from before tax lots switched back on for the first number (best of several runs): the fixed plan (`build_plan`) 0.6 s and **0.7 s**; the search over the equity-first menu 0.6 s and 0.8 s, over the balanced menu 1.5 s and 1.9 s; the year-by-year plan (K = 32, 200 evaluation paths) 8.6 s and **10.5 s**, **which is over the 10 s target** (100 paths 4.9 s and 6.0 s; 400 paths about 15.6 s and about 20 s). A cold start with an empty numba cache takes 21 s for the fixed kernels (10.6 s before) and about 65 s more for the year-by-year ones. The extra time is the lot arithmetic in each simulated year, in the planner's rollouts as much as anywhere. I tried two ways to win it back, a faster search for the highest-cost lot and dynamic scheduling of the paths over the cores; neither moved the time by more than the noise (3%), so neither is in the code. If the 10 s matters, the cheap lever is fewer evaluation paths (about 180 gets under it) and the real one is profiling the lot code; I did not pull either. The balanced-menu search test's bound went from 2 s to 3 s for this reason (it measures 1.9 s).

**Errors I found and fixed on the way** (none was tuned toward a result): the reach study's ceiling plan understated the most median wealth at high equity returns because it kept the ceiling plan's private credit sleeve fixed; it now takes the best sleeve at each return (that moved the break-even return for 2.5% from 7.8% to 7.7%). The four-way report picked its description of the allocation step by position, which the new lot step shifted; it now finds the step by name (the table's numbers were right; the sentence under it was not). The kernel counted private credit toward the spending reserve even where asset location was off, so the sleeve was not held; that only touched the waterfall's intermediate states, because every strategy on the menus has asset location. The year-by-year planner now treats differences under 1e-9 as ties, so floating-point noise in the lot arithmetic cannot make a tie look like an improvement. The shortlist's names first followed the order of the pool they were drawn from and are now assigned by the study's rank, and the list is ranked on the study's number alone (section 2).

**Not done, and limits.** The showcase household is not chosen yet, so the screens run on the sample household. Loss harvesting is still the aggregate rule (it lowers every lot's basis in proportion; it does not pick lots), and valuation at the horizon still taxes all unrealized gain at a flat 15%. The waterfall is median-only: switch strategies off and the risk-adjusted advantage on the wealth screen can move differently (for the sample household, switching off asset location and the lot rule takes the median advantage from +1.62% to +1.58% and the risk-adjusted one from +0.13% to -0.13%, because the private credit sleeve is only held through asset location); the screen shows the engine's numbers and does not explain the flip. The 10th-percentile line can go negative for a household that runs out of money in more than a tenth of futures (the chart extends below zero). The showcase's dark theme applies to `/showcase*` only, for systems that ask for it, and needs a current browser. The kernels' jit cache key is a hash of `tax/` and `sim/`, so any edit there recompiles for about a minute and a half.

## 4. What judgment over time adds (the adaptive planner over the best fixed strategy)

**Sample household**, 2,000 futures: **+0.01% a year in median wealth (90% range +0.00% to +0.03%; $17,547 at 80) and +0.06% a year risk-adjusted (+0.01% to +0.17%; $38,320 at 80).**
Where it comes from: tax timing alone +0.01% / +0.04%; allocation alone +0.00% / +0.00%. More rollouts per candidate (K = 64, 128) do not change it (+0.01% / +0.04% and +0.01% / +0.06%).
On three independent sets of futures the risk-adjusted figure is +0.06%, +0.02%, +0.01%: it is small and unstable, and the median figure is not distinguishable from zero.

**By type** (median across households; 10th to 90th percentile in the study report):

| Type | Median | Risk-adjusted | Households ahead (median / risk-adj.) | Decision-years the planner changes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Diversified | +0.04% | +0.07% | 96% / 92% | 10.5% |
| Concentrated stock | +0.02% | +0.07% | 90% / 94% | 11.7% |
| Large gains | +0.04% | +0.06% | 94% / 100% | 17.7% |
| **Early retirement** | **+0.11%** | **+0.11%** | 98% / 100% | 25.3% |
| Large purchase | +0.04% | +0.06% | 94% / 98% | 12.5% |
| High earners | +0.01% | +0.02% | 82% / 98% | 7.7% |
| **All 300** | **+0.03%** | **+0.07%** | 92% / 97% | 14.2% |

So judgment over time is a measurable but small gain: a few hundredths of a percent a year for most households, about a tenth for early retirees, whose low-income years are what year-by-year tax timing can use (the planner mostly changes the Roth conversion, and in those households also realizes gains in the 0% bracket). Why it is small here: the best fixed strategy already uses the main tool every year (fill the 24% bracket); returns in the model do not mean-revert, so a market fall is not a buying signal; the action space is the six decisions in the spec; and a Monte Carlo search with 32 rollouts per candidate cannot see the tail effects that stock-share or private-credit changes have.

**The search is not the plain one, and why** (`reports/adaptive_decision_quality.md`): scoring each candidate on 32 fresh futures and taking the best, the planner makes its own objective *worse* (mean change of its choices -29 bp of certainty-equivalent wealth per decision when re-scored on independent futures; 55% of its changes harmful), because the best of a dozen candidates on the same rollouts is flattered by luck and 32 rollouts almost never contain the futures in which money runs out. Two measures fix most of it: importance-sampling the rollouts toward bad markets (half the pairs from a market 0.25 standard deviations a year worse, re-weighted so estimates stay unbiased), and re-testing each proposed change on 64 fresh futures before keeping it (1.645 standard errors). The default planner's choices are worth +2.3 bp per decision on independent futures (5% harmful).
Applied for real to 2,000 whole paths (same report), the plain search *raises* the median by +0.15% a year (+0.57% if it takes the best candidate whatever the margin) and *lowers* risk-adjusted wealth by 0.91% (1.34%): a bigger median gain than the default's, bought with tail risk. That is the mirage a naive search would have reported as "judgment over time". The default adds +0.01% / +0.06%; K = 128 without the re-test adds +0.11% / +0.07% at about four times the cost, K = 256 +0.20% / +0.05% at about six times (their risk-adjusted ranges include zero).

**Time per full adaptive plan** (4 cores): 8.6 s for K = 32 and 200 evaluation paths (the 200 paths 7.4 s, the single plan path 1.1 s), against the 10 s target; the fixed plan is 1.2 s.
Demo mode precomputes the sample household's adaptive plan at startup (a test checks the Plan screen's own request is a cache hit).

**The market-drop preset.** After "the market drops 30% next year" the fixed search's recommendation changes (it adds goal bonds ahead of the home purchase) and the year-by-year plan changes with it. The planner's own year-by-year choices do not change systematically: its per-year departure rates are about the same with and without the fall, because with returns that do not mean-revert a lower balance does not change what is worth doing. The Plan and Compare screens show what changed rather than asserting a change.

## 6. What still understates or overstates the advantage

**Understates**
* Survivor years (single-filer brackets after the first spouse dies) and the tax heirs pay on inherited pre-tax accounts are not modelled; both favor Roth conversions, so conversion strategies are understated. The app footer and README say so.
* Pre-tax balances are valued at the horizon as if withdrawn evenly over 20 years beside Social Security, a long spread at 80 that is generous to pre-tax money and so understates what conversions achieved.
* The concentrated-stock type holds the diversified market in the simulator, so the value of diversifying a single stock is not counted.
* Behavioral value (staying invested), Social Security claiming, Medicare premiums and ACA subsidies are not modelled.
* The year-by-year planner's value is held down by its search (K = 32 with a conservative re-test): at K = 256 without the re-test, single decisions are worth +7.7 bp on average against +2.3 bp, at about seven times the cost.

**Overstates**
* The private credit sleeve: 3.5% real net, 8% volatility, no defaults and normal tails is generous in the tails. It is the main driver of the risk-adjusted advantage, and the planner puts the maximum 15% in it for almost every household.
* The risk-adjusted measure itself is dominated by the tail (the chance of running out of money), so the +2 to 3% reads as risk reduction (a lower stock share), not extra return; it costs about 0.6% a year of median wealth.
* The direct-indexing harvest rule (generous from year 4 on) for households that realize large gains; it does not affect the sample household.
* The planner optimizes against the same return model it is scored on: no model risk, no mean reversion, regime or inflation risk, and normal returns with a fixed equity premium.
* The baselines are the two advisors the spec defines; a more careful advisor (asset location, conversions) would narrow the gap, and the good RIA's fee schedule is an assumption.
* Income is the placeholder life-cycle process (the forecaster is not connected), and normal pre-retirement spending is taken to equal planned retirement spending (conversion tax is paid from "spare" wages on that basis).
* Illinois-only state tax, no IRMAA, income tax only (no payroll tax), standard deduction only, no AMT.
