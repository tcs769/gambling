## Update 2: the spending reserve, and the app now plans equity-first

You answered that the demo should "maximize equity holding while minimizing the need to liquidate equity", so that a good part of the expenses is paid from private credit and bonds and as much of the rest as possible is equity, and that the specified assumptions should stay as they are. That is built and is now the default. **The result is closer to your target and still short of it: +1.61% a year in median wealth for the sample household (+1.62% for the typical household of the 300), not 2 to 3%. The risk-adjusted advantage fell from +2.54% to +0.14%.**

**What was built.** A spending reserve is the eighth strategy choice. Bonds and private credit hold the next 2, 4 or 6 years of what the household will need to take out of the portfolio (retirement spending net of Social Security; the private credit sleeve counts toward the reserve), the goals of those years sit in the taxable goal bucket, and every other dollar is in stocks, with no glide. Expenses, goals, taxes and required distributions are paid from bonds and private credit before stocks, inside each account, so stocks are rarely sold; after a year in which stocks fell nothing is sold to build or refill the reserve. The equity-first planning style (1,008 strategies: reserve, conversions, private credit, direct indexing, 401(k) type, goal funding) is the default; the balanced style is unchanged and still available through the API (the screens have no switch yet). Strategies without a reserve run bit for bit as before (a test compares them). No assumption was changed.

## Update: the equity question, and what 2 to 3% would take

After this report you said the raw advantage should be 2 to 3% and that the plan holds too little equity. Measured (`reports/equity_share.md`, `reports/path_to_2_3_percent.md`, `reports/household_study_risk_aversion_{3,2,1}/`); at that point nothing in the app's behavior had changed and none of the changes below was adopted (the spending reserve in Update 2 came after your answers to the questions at its end). Everything in this update is the balanced style.

**You were right about the equity.** The plan holds 42.5% of the portfolio in stocks falling to 21% from 70 (a 50% stock share of the money outside a 15% private credit sleeve); the good advisor holds 80% falling to 50%. The planner's objective (risk aversion 3, a $50,000 floor) is decided by the futures in which money runs out, since one such future counts as much as about 3,600 ordinary ones, so it picks the lowest stock share on the menu. More equity raises the raw advantage by about 0.25 points a year for every 10 points of stock share and lowers the risk-adjusted one. | Risk aversion 2 | 51% then 30% | +0.53% | +1.70% | 0.1% | $1.57M |

The typical household of the 300, searched at the same three settings: raw +0.35%, +0.94% and +1.54%; risk-adjusted +2.19%, +1.46% and +0.38%; holding 51%, 68% and 85% in stocks today and running out of money in 0.6%, 0.8% and 1.9% of futures (the advisor 2.2%). By type at risk aversion 1 the raw advantage is +1.38% to +1.72%; the large-purchase type runs out of money more often than the advisor there (2.7% against 1.3%).

**How much of that is skill.** Run at the advisor's own allocation, the plan's tools (fee, asset location, conversions, withdrawal order, goal funding) are worth **+0.83%** a year for the sample household and +0.78%, +0.80% and +0.81% for the typical household of the 300 (10th to 90th percentile across households +0.64% to +1.01%). The raw advantage also equals the extra yearly return the advisor's portfolio would need, to within 0.02 points, so how it is annualized is not what holds it down.

| **All 300** | **+0.03%** | **+0.07%** | 92% / 97% | 14.2% |

So judgment over time is a measurable but small gain: a few hundredths of a percent a year for most households, about a tenth for early retirees, whose low-income years are what year-by-year tax timing can use (the planner mostly changes the Roth conversion, and in those households also realizes gains in the 0% bracket). Why it is small here: the best fixed strategy already uses the main tool every year (fill the 24% bracket); returns in the model do not mean-revert, so a market fall is not a buying signal; the action space is the six decisions in the spec; and a Monte Carlo search with 32 rollouts per candidate cannot see the tail effects that stock-share or private-credit changes have.

**The search is not the plain one, and why** (`reports/adaptive_decision_quality.md`): scoring each candidate on 32 fresh futures and taking the best, the planner makes its own objective *worse* (mean change of its choices -29 bp of certainty-equivalent wealth per decision when re-scored on independent futures; 55% of its changes harmful), because the best of a dozen candidates on the same rollouts is flattered by luck and 32 rollouts almost never contain the futures in which money runs out. Two measures fix most of it: importance-sampling the rollouts toward bad markets (half the pairs from a market 0.25 standard deviations a year worse, re-weighted so estimates stay unbiased), and re-testing each proposed change on 64 fresh futures before keeping it (1.645 standard errors). The default planner's choices are worth +2.3 bp per decision on independent futures (5% harmful).
Applied for real to 2,000 whole paths (same report), the plain search *raises* the median by +0.15% a year (+0.57% if it takes the best candidate whatever the margin) and *lowers* risk-adjusted wealth by 0.91% (1.34%): a bigger median gain than the default's, bought with tail risk. That is the mirage a naive search would have reported as "judgment over time". The default adds +0.01% / +0.06%; K = 128 without the re-test adds +0.11% / +0.07% at about four times the cost, K = 256 +0.20% / +0.05% at about six times (their risk-adjusted ranges include zero).

**Time per full adaptive plan** (4 cores): 8.6 s for K = 32 and 200 evaluation paths (the 200 paths 7.4 s, the single plan path 1.1 s), against the 10 s target; the fixed plan is 1.2 s.
Demo mode precomputes the sample household's adaptive plan at startup (a test checks the Plan screen's own request is a cache hit).

**The market-drop preset.** After "the market drops 30% next year" the fixed search's recommendation changes (it adds goal bonds ahead of the home purchase) and the year-by-year plan changes with it. The planner's own year-by-year choices do not change systematically: its per-year departure rates are about the same with and without the fall, because with returns that do not mean-revert a lower balance does not change what is worth doing. The Plan and Compare screens show what changed rather than asserting a change.

## 6. What still understates or overstates the advantage

**Understates**
* Survivor years (single-filer brackets after the first spouse dies) and the tax heirs pay on inherited pre-tax accounts are not modelled; both favor Roth conversions, so conversion strategies are understated. The app footer and README say so.
* Pre-tax balances are valued at the horizon as if withdrawn evenly over 20 years beside Social Security, a long spread at 80 that is generous to pre-tax money and so understates what conversions achieved.
* The concentrated-stock type holds the diversified market in the simulator, so the value of diversifying a single stock is not counted.
* Behavioral value (staying invested), Social Security claiming, Medicare premiums and ACA subsidies are not modelled.
* The year-by-year planner's value is held down by its search (K = 32 with a conservative re-test): at K = 256 without the re-test, single decisions are worth +7.7 bp on average against +2.3 bp, at about seven times the cost.

**Overstates**
* The private credit sleeve: 3.5% real net, 8% volatility, no defaults and normal tails is generous in the tails. It is the main driver of the risk-adjusted advantage, and the planner puts the maximum 15% in it for almost every household.
* The risk-adjusted measure itself is dominated by the tail (the chance of running out of money), so the +2 to 3% reads as risk reduction (a lower stock share), not extra return; it costs about 0.6% a year of median wealth.
* The direct-indexing harvest rule (generous from year 4 on) for households that realize large gains; it does not affect the sample household.
* The planner optimizes against the same return model it is scored on: no model risk, no mean reversion, regime or inflation risk, and normal returns with a fixed equity premium.
* The baselines are the two advisors the spec defines; a more careful advisor (asset location, conversions) would narrow the gap, and the good RIA's fee schedule is an assumption.
* Income is the placeholder life-cycle process (the forecaster is not connected), and normal pre-retirement spending is taken to equal planned retirement spending (conversion tax is paid from "spare" wages on that basis).
* Illinois-only state tax, no IRMAA, income tax only (no payroll tax), standard deduction only, no AMT.
